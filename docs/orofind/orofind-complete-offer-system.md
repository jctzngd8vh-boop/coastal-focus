## Before You Start: How This System Works

### What you're holding

This is a finished offer system for an expert service business. It has the offer itself, the page that sells it, the emails that warm people up and close them, and the ascension stack that turns one sale into a client relationship worth three to five times the first invoice.

Everything is built on one structure. It works across coaching, consulting, agency work, specialist freelance work and local professional services:

1. **A paid front door (The Diagnostic).** A small, fast yes. The buyer pays you to find out exactly what's wrong and what to do about it.
2. **A fixed-scope core engagement (The Build).** The main result, delivered over a set window with a defined finish line.
3. **A continuity tier (Partner).** Keeps the result in place and keeps the revenue coming in after the Build ends.

### Why this structure wins

Most service businesses sell one thing. A big engagement, pitched on a free call, to someone who met them twenty minutes ago. That's a $10,000 decision with nothing in between, and most people say "let me think about it" because that's the only safe answer available to them.

The paid Diagnostic splits that into two smaller decisions. It does five jobs at once:

- It filters out people who were never going to buy. Someone who won't pay $950 to fix a $15,000-a-month problem was never going to pay $9,500.
- It gets you paid for the thinking you currently give away on free discovery calls.
- It lets the buyer experience how you work before the big commitment, which is the single biggest driver of trust.
- It gives you the information you need to write a proposal that's almost impossible to say no to, because it's built on their numbers.
- It creates a natural, low-pressure moment to present the Build: "Here's what we found. Here's what fixing it looks like. Want us to do it with you?"

Operators who run this model commonly report Diagnostic-to-Build conversion of 40% to 70%, against 15% to 30% for a free discovery call that goes straight to a big proposal. Your numbers will be your own. Section 7 (Part Two) shows you how to track them from day one.

### How to finish this system (three passes)

**Pass 1: 60 to 90 minutes.** Fill in the Token Key below. Use your actual clients, actual numbers and actual language. If you have to guess at a token, mark it and go ask three past clients before you publish anything.

**Pass 2: half a day.** Find-and-replace every token across the document. Then read every asset out loud. Cut any sentence that isn't true for you. Change any phrase you wouldn't say to a client's face. The copy is written to survive this pass. Most of it will.

**Pass 3: launch.** Follow the priority order in Section 8. The short version: get the Diagnostic offer live first, then the sales page, then emails 1 to 15, then the ascension stack.

### Three rules that protect you

1. **Never publish a result you haven't produced.** Wherever the copy calls for proof and you don't have it yet, use the Founding Client version printed beside it. Thin proof told honestly sells better than thick proof that smells invented.
2. **Never invent scarcity.** Every deadline and cap in this system is tied to a real limit: your calendar. Keep it that way. Fake countdown timers cost you the trust that makes the $10,000 sale possible.
3. **Don't add deliverables to justify the price.** When a buyer pushes back, sharpen the outcome. More stuff makes an offer harder to understand and harder to deliver.

- Lines that begin with **Operator note:** are instructions for you. Delete them before you publish.
- Everything else is finished copy. It's written to be published.

### Token 1: `{Buyer}`

**What it is:** who you serve, written in the plural.
**Rule:** industry + size or stage + role. Specific enough that a stranger could find ten of them on LinkedIn in five minutes. Never "business owners," "entrepreneurs," "high achievers" or "anyone who wants to grow."
**Test:** could a buyer read it and say "that's me" or "that's not me" in under two seconds?

- Clinic Coach: physical therapy clinic owners with one to three locations
- Sales Consultant: founders of 5 to 30 person B2B service firms
- Dental Ads Agency: dental groups with 3 to 12 locations
- Email Copywriter: Shopify brands doing $1M to $10M a year
- Physician Planner: physicians in their first ten years of practice

### Token 2: `{Trigger}`

**What it is:** the event that happened right before your buyer started looking for help. Written as a clause that reads naturally after the words "usually right after."
**Rule:** an event with a date attached, not a feeling. "Frustrated with growth" is a feeling. "Lost their biggest client in March" is a trigger.
**Why it matters:** without a trigger, you're asking someone to change on a random Tuesday for no reason. With one, you're showing up at the exact moment they're already looking.

- Clinic Coach: a therapist gives notice and the schedule suddenly has 30 open slots a week
- Sales Consultant: the founder realizes they've personally closed every deal for three years
- Dental Ads Agency: a new location opens and the phones don't ring
- Email Copywriter: Meta ad costs jump and email suddenly has to carry more of the revenue
- Physician Planner: the first attending paycheck lands in the same week as the first loan statement

### Token 3: `{Pain Phrase}`

**What it is:** the problem in the exact words your buyer uses. Harvested from sales calls, reviews, forums, DMs and the first five minutes of every discovery call. Written as a direct quote.
**Rule:** if it sounds like a textbook, it's wrong. It should sound like something said at 9pm to a spouse or a business partner.
**Test:** would a buyer read this and think "who told you about us?"

- Clinic Coach: "We're busy, but there are still holes in the schedule every single week."
- Sales Consultant: "If I stop selling, the pipeline stops. Nobody else here can close."
- Dental Ads Agency: "We spend $40k a month on ads and I honestly couldn't tell you what it's buying."
- Email Copywriter: "We have 80,000 people on our list and we email them twice a month, when somebody remembers."
- Physician Planner: "I make more than I ever have and I somehow don't feel any further ahead."

### Token 4: `{Outcome}`

**What it is:** the measurable result you deliver. Written as a verb phrase that reads naturally after the word "to."
**Rule:** a number, a unit and a direction. If you couldn't measure it on the last day of the engagement, rewrite it.
**Test:** read it after "help you." "Help you add 40 patient visits a week" works. "Help you scale" doesn't say anything.

- Clinic Coach: add 40 patient visits a week
- Sales Consultant: get two non-founder reps closing deals on their own
- Dental Ads Agency: get cost per new patient under $250
- Email Copywriter: make email 30% of store revenue
- Physician Planner: invest 20% of your income while paying down your loans on schedule

### Token 5: `{Timeframe}`

**What it is:** the length of the core engagement and the window for the first measurable result.
**Default:** 90 days.
**Rule:** the shortest window in which you can produce a visible, measurable result. Not the full transformation. The first result you can point at. If the honest answer is six months, sell a 90-day Build with a first-result milestone at day 90 and sell the rest through Partner.

- Clinic Coach: 90 days
- Sales Consultant: 90 days
- Dental Ads Agency: 60 days
- Email Copywriter: 90 days
- Physician Planner: 60 days

**What it is:** the name of your method. The way you get the result that's different from what they've tried.
**Rule:** it names the mechanic, not the feeling. Two to four words. A buyer should roughly understand what it does the first time they hear it. "The 3-Pass Filing System" beats "The Prosperity Method" every time.

## 1.0 The Offer in One Sentence

Before anything else, your offer has to survive this test:

> **We help `{Buyer}` `{Outcome}` in `{Timeframe}` through the `{Mechanism}`, without `{Dread}`.**

If it takes more than one sentence, or the sentence needs the words "and also," the offer is trying to do too many jobs. Split it or cut it.

**Three checks before you move on:**

1. **The stranger check.** Read it to someone outside your industry. Can they repeat back who it's for and what it does? If not, it's too clever.
2. **The buyer check.** Read it to a past client. Do they say "yes, that's exactly what happened" or do they correct you? Their correction is your better sentence.
3. **The competitor check.** Could your top competitor put their name on this sentence and have it still be true? If yes, your `{Mechanism}` or `{Outcome}` isn't specific enough. Section 5 (Part Two) goes deep on this.

## 1.1 The Grand Slam Offer

A grand slam offer is one where the buyer feels stupid saying no. Not because of pressure, but because the value is so obviously larger than the price, and the risk is so obviously handled, that "no" costs them more than "yes."

It's built from six parts. We'll write each one.

### 1.1.1 The Promise

The promise is the specific result you commit to, stated plainly enough that the buyer could hold you to it.

**The promise formula:**

> In `{Timeframe}`, we'll help you `{Outcome}`, without `{Dread}`. If you do the work and we don't get you there, `[guarantee from 1.1.6]`.

**What makes a promise strong:**

- **A number.** "More clients" is a wish. "Four new retainer clients" is a promise.
- **A window.** No timeframe means no accountability, and buyers notice.
- **A condition.** "If you do the work" isn't a weasel clause. It tells the buyer you're serious and they have a role. Buyers trust conditional promises more than unconditional ones, because unconditional promises sound like lies.
- **The dread removed.** Half of the promise is what they won't have to do.

**Weak vs. strong, side by side:**

- Weak: "Grow your practice with proven systems."
  Strong: "Add 40 patient visits a week in 90 days, without working Saturdays."
- Weak: "Build a sales team that sells."
  Strong: "Get two non-founder reps closing on their own in 90 days, so you can take your name off the sales calendar."
- Weak: "Maximize your ad performance."
  Strong: "Get cost per new patient under $250 in 60 days, without raising your ad budget a dollar."
- Weak: "Email marketing that converts."
  Strong: "Make email 30% of your store's revenue in 90 days, without emailing your list every day."
- Weak: "Financial clarity for physicians."
  Strong: "Invest 20% of your income starting in the next 60 days while paying your loans down on schedule, without living like a resident."

### 1.1.2 The Value Equation, Applied

Perceived value comes down to four levers. Two you push up, two you push down.

> **Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort and Sacrifice)**

Here's exactly how this offer pulls each lever. Use these moves in your copy, your calls and your delivery.

**Lever 1: Make the dream outcome bigger (without exaggerating).**
- Name the second-order result, not just the first. Not "40 more visits a week." Say "40 more visits a week, which is about $290,000 a year at your current rates, which is the third location you've been sketching on napkins."
- Name the status change. Who do they become when this works? The founder who takes a real vacation. The clinic other owners call for advice. The planner's client who stops checking their account balance before dinner.
- Use their numbers, not yours. A result stated in their revenue, their schedule and their dollars always feels bigger than a generic one.

**Lever 2: Raise the perceived likelihood it works for them.**
- Show the mechanism. People believe what they understand. Explain why it works in two sentences (1.1.3).
- Show proof from someone like them. Same industry, similar size. One matching case study beats ten mismatched ones.
- Make the guarantee specific (1.1.6). A guarantee is a likelihood signal. It says "I'm confident enough to pay for being wrong."
- Show the plan. A week-by-week map makes the result feel inevitable instead of hoped-for.
- Disqualify loudly. "This isn't for you if..." makes everyone who stays feel more certain it's for them.

**Lever 3: Shrink the time delay.**
- Promise a first win inside 14 days, separate from the main outcome. In the Build, that's the Quick-Win Fix delivered in Phase 1. Buyers forgive a long road if they get something on day 10.
- Tell them exactly what happens in the first 48 hours after they pay (1.4.3). Silence after a payment is the single biggest cause of buyer's remorse in service businesses.
- Show the timeline in weeks, not months. "Week 3" feels closer than "Month 1."

**Lever 4: Shrink the effort and sacrifice.**
- Do the parts they hate. If your buyer hates writing, you write. If they hate setup, you set up. Done-with-you beats done-yourself on every sales page in this category.
- Cap their time commitment and say the number. "About 2 hours a week, including our calls." Vague time commitments sound like unlimited time commitments.
- Remove the `{Dread}` explicitly. Say what they won't have to do, out loud, more than once.
- Hand them finished assets, not homework. Templates already filled in. Scripts already written. Dashboards already built.

### 1.1.3 The Mechanism

The mechanism is the reason your approach works when the other things they tried didn't. It's what turns you from a vendor into a method.

**The mechanism explanation formula (use this word for word on calls and pages):**

> Most `{Buyer}` try `{Old Way}`. It doesn't work because it treats the symptom: `[the visible problem]`. The real problem is `[the underlying cause]`. The `{Mechanism}` fixes that first, which is why the results stick.

**Filled examples:**

- **Clinic Coach:** "Most clinic owners try discount promos and posting on Instagram. It doesn't work because it treats the symptom, which is not enough new patients. The real problem is that existing patients drop off after visit four and nobody notices. The Full Schedule System fixes the plan-of-care drop-off first, which is why the schedule fills from patients you already have."
- **Sales Consultant:** "Most founders try hiring a closer and hoping they figure it out. It doesn't work because it treats the symptom, which is not enough sales capacity. The real problem is that the sales process lives in the founder's head. The Founder-Out Sales Install gets it out of your head and onto paper first, which is why new reps can actually close."
- **Dental Ads Agency:** "Most groups try agencies that report clicks instead of patients. It doesn't work because it treats the symptom, which is not enough leads. The real problem is that 40% to 60% of the leads you already pay for never get booked. The Cost-Per-Patient Rebuild fixes lead-to-booking first, which is why cost per patient drops without more spend."

**The default four-phase mechanism.** Every Build in this system runs on the same four phases. Rename them to fit your world, but keep the structure. It's what makes the offer feel like a method instead of "some calls."

**Phase 1: Map (Weeks 1 to 2).**
- What happens: the Diagnostic findings get turned into a written plan. We confirm the baseline numbers, the target numbers and the three to five changes that will move them.
- Deliverable: the `{Mechanism}` Blueprint, a written plan with the baseline, target, weekly milestones and who owns each task.
- Their job: send access, data and one 60-minute working session.
- The win they feel: the Quick-Win Fix. One change we can make in the first 14 days that produces a visible result. For the Clinic Coach, it's a recall list of every patient who dropped off in the last 90 days, plus the script to call them. For the Email Copywriter, it's one welcome flow rewritten and live. For the Sales Consultant, it's the founder's top 20 open deals reviewed with next steps written for each.

**Phase 2: Build (Weeks 3 to 6).**
- What happens: we build the core assets the result depends on. Systems, scripts, templates, campaigns, documents.
- Deliverable: the finished assets, built with them in working sessions and finished by us between sessions.
- Their job: two working sessions per week, maximum 60 minutes each, plus review of anything we send within 3 business days.
- The win they feel: something real exists that didn't exist before. They can see it, open it and use it.

**Phase 3: Install (Weeks 7 to 10).**
- What happens: the assets go live in their business. Their team gets trained. We watch the first real uses and fix what breaks.
- Deliverable: the live system, a recorded training for their team, and written standard operating procedures.
- Their job: one 60-minute working session per week, put the team in the room for training, and use the system for real.
- The win they feel: the numbers start moving and they're not the one doing all the work.

**Phase 4: Prove (Weeks 11 to 13).**
- What happens: we measure the result against the baseline, tighten what's underperforming and write the plan for the next 12 months.
- Deliverable: the Day-90 Results Review, a written comparison of baseline vs. now, plus the 12-month roadmap.
- Their job: one 90-minute review session.
- The win they feel: a number on a page that proves it worked. And a clear answer to "what's next," which is where the Partner tier comes in (Section 4).

**Naming rules for your mechanism:**

1. Describe the mechanic. A buyer should get a rough sense of what it does from the name alone.
2. Two to four words. Three is the sweet spot.
3. Avoid words every competitor uses: "Accelerator," "Blueprint" (we use it only as a deliverable name), "Mastery," "Elite," "Method" on its own.
4. Say it out loud in a sentence: "We'll run you through the ___." If it sounds silly spoken, it'll sound silly on a sales call.
5. Check it isn't trademarked in your industry. Search it in quotes plus your industry.

### 1.1.5 Pricing Recommendation Range

**Step 1: Set the price from the value of the outcome, not your hours.**

The core rule: **your Build price should be 10% to 20% of the first-year value of the outcome to the buyer.** That gives them a 5× to 10× return, which is the range where a rational buyer says yes without much agonizing.

Worked example (Clinic Coach): 40 more visits a week × $140 per visit × 50 weeks = $280,000 a year in new revenue. At a 30% margin, that's $84,000 in profit. 10% to 20% of the revenue value is $28,000 to $56,000. Of the profit value, $8,400 to $16,800. Start conservative and use the profit number: the Build lands between $8,400 and $16,800. The reference price is $7,500, which is deliberately under the range for a coach with thin proof. Raise it with every five clients (see 1.5).

Worked example (Sales Consultant): two reps each closing $60,000 a quarter in new contracts = $480,000 a year in revenue the founder didn't personally close. 10% of that is $48,000. A $18,000 Build is a 26× revenue return. That's safe, and leaves room to raise.

**Step 2: Check it against your floor.** Your floor is what the Build costs you to deliver, plus the margin you need. Total your hours across the stack (about 80 to 90 hours at full delivery), multiply by your loaded hourly cost, and add 50% margin. If the value-based number lands below your floor, the problem isn't price. It's that you're serving buyers whose outcome isn't worth enough. Move up-market (Token 1) before you raise the price.

**Step 3: Pick your numbers from these ranges.**

| Offer | Price range | Default | Credit rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Diagnostic | $500 to $2,500 | 8% to 15% of `{Core Price}` | 100% credited toward any Build tier started within 14 days |
| Blueprint (self-implement tier) | 35% to 45% of `{Core Price}` | 40% | Diagnostic credited |
| Build (flagship) | $5,000 to $25,000 | Value-based, see Step 1 | Diagnostic credited |
| Build + Run (premium tier) | 2× to 2.5× `{Core Price}` | 2.2× | Diagnostic credited |
| Partner (continuity) | 15% to 25% of `{Core Price}` per month (lower where flat-fee norms exist, as in financial planning) | 20% | Month-to-month after a 3-month minimum |
| Self-Install Kit (low-ticket downsell) | $97 to $497 | $297 | 100% credited toward the Diagnostic within 30 days |

**Reference prices for the five example businesses:**

| Business | Diagnostic | Blueprint | Build | Build + Run | Partner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clinic Coach | $950 | $3,000 | $7,500 | $16,500 | $1,500/mo |
| Sales Consultant | $2,500 | $7,500 | $18,000 | $40,000 | $4,500/mo |
| Dental Ads Agency | $1,500 | $4,800 | $12,000 | $26,500 | $3,000/mo + spend |
| Email Copywriter | $750 | $3,600 | $9,000 | $19,800 | $2,000/mo |
| Physician Planner | $600 | $1,800 | $4,500 | $9,900 | $400/mo |

**Payment structure for the Build:**
- **Pay in full:** `{Core Price}`.
- **Three monthly payments:** each payment is 36% of `{Core Price}`, for a total of 108%. (On $9,000, that's three payments of $3,240, a total of $9,720.)
- Never offer more than three payments on a 90-day engagement. If the final payment falls after the work ends, you'll spend month four chasing it.

**Step 4: Present it with an anchor.** Covered in full in 1.5. The short version: always show the cost of the problem before the price, and always show the premium tier before the flagship.

### 1.1.6 The Guarantee and Risk Reversal

The buyer's real question is never "is this good?" It's "what happens to me if this doesn't work?" Answer it before they ask.

This system uses a **two-guarantee structure:** one on the front door, one on the Build. Each removes the risk at the moment it's highest.

**Guarantee 1: The Diagnostic Guarantee (unconditional)**

Copy, ready to use:

> **The "Worth It or It's Free" Guarantee.** At the end of your Diagnostic, if you don't believe the findings are worth at least three times what you paid, tell me before we hang up. I'll refund every dollar, and you keep the Findings Report anyway. No forms, no questions, no awkward follow-up email.

Why it works: the Diagnostic is the riskiest moment for a buyer who's never worked with you. Making it unconditional removes that risk completely. And because the buyer has to decide before the call ends (while they're looking at the findings), almost nobody takes you up on it. Operators running this typically see refund rates under 5%.

Cost math: on a $950 Diagnostic with a 5% refund rate, the guarantee costs you about $48 per Diagnostic sold. If it lifts your booking rate by even 10%, it's paid for itself many times over.

**Guarantee 2: The Build Guarantee (conditional outcome)**

Copy, ready to use:

> **The "We Keep Going" Guarantee.** If you do your part (show up to the sessions, complete your tasks within 7 days, and put the system in front of your team) and we don't hit the target we agree on in your Blueprint by day 90, we keep working with you at no cost until we do, for up to 60 more days. If we still haven't hit it by then, we'll refund your final payment.

Operator note: the target in the guarantee is the one written into the Blueprint in Week 2. Not the headline number on your sales page. Set it with the client, based on their baseline. This protects you from guaranteeing a number you haven't seen data for yet.

**The conditions, written for the client agreement:**
1. Attend at least 10 of the 12 working sessions, or reschedule with 24 hours' notice.
2. Complete assigned tasks within 7 days of the session where they're assigned.
3. Give us access to the data, tools and team members named in the Blueprint within 5 business days of request.
4. Use the system as built for at least 30 days before Day 90.
5. Track the scorecard weekly (we'll do most of this for you).

**Cost math (price it like insurance):** say 1 in 8 clients triggers the extension, and the average extension takes 10 hours of your time. At $250 an hour, that's $2,500 per triggered client. Spread across 8 clients, the guarantee costs you about $313 per Build sold. On a $9,000 Build, that's 3.5% of revenue. And a buyer reading "we keep going until it works" is noticeably more likely to say yes. That's a trade you take every time.

**Alternative guarantees, if the default doesn't fit your model:**

- **Timeline guarantee** (best for agencies and freelancers who deliver assets): "Your `[first major deliverable]` is live by day 21, or your second payment is waived."
- **Pilot with a clean exit** (best when you have no proof yet): "Start with the first 30 days. If you don't want to continue after the Map phase, walk away, keep the Blueprint, and pay nothing more."
- **Performance fee** (only if you control delivery and can measure the outcome cleanly): "Lower base fee plus a bonus of `$X` when we hit the target." Use carefully. Attribution fights will cost you more than the bonus is worth.
- **The Founding Client guarantee** (when you have zero case studies): "You're one of my first five clients on this program. You get the full Build at 40% off. In exchange, I ask for honest feedback, a detailed results write-up and a recorded testimonial if (and only if) it works. If it doesn't, you get the We Keep Going Guarantee like everyone else."

**What never to guarantee:**
- Anything you don't control. An agency can't guarantee revenue if the client's sales team won't call leads. A coach can't guarantee profit if the client won't raise prices.
- "Satisfaction." It defines nothing, so it reverses no risk.
- A number you haven't seen the baseline for. That's why the Build guarantee is tied to the Blueprint, not the sales page.

**Turning a thin proof stack into a strong one.** If you have little or no proof, say so, sell the pilot, and use it to make proof. Here's the order of strength to work toward:

1. Named client results with numbers
2. Anonymized results with numbers ("a 7-location dental group cut cost per new patient from $410 to $236")
3. Your process shown openly (screenshots, the actual checklist, a sample Findings Report with details removed)
4. Credentials, licenses, certifications
5. Volume ("214 Diagnostics completed")
6. Third-party validation (reviews, press, partnerships)

Collect proof on purpose: at the Day-90 Results Review, ask for a testimonial while the before-and-after numbers are on the screen. That's when it's easiest for them to say yes and easiest to say something specific.

## 1.2 The Positioning Statement

Positioning is the decision about what you want to be known for, and by whom. The statement is the internal version you build everything from. The one-liners are the external versions people actually hear.

### The full positioning statement (internal)

> For `{Buyer}` who `[are feeling the pain right after the Trigger]`, `{Brand}` is the `[narrow category]` that helps them `{Outcome}` in `{Timeframe}`. Unlike `{Old Way}`, we use the `{Mechanism}`, which fixes `[the underlying cause]` first, so the result holds after we leave.

**Filled (Dental Ads Agency):**

> For dental groups with 3 to 12 locations who just opened a new office and can't get the phones to ring, `[Brand]` is the patient acquisition agency that helps them get cost per new patient under $250 in 60 days. Unlike agencies that report clicks instead of patients, we use the Cost-Per-Patient Rebuild, which fixes lead-to-booking first, so the result holds after we leave.

**Choosing your narrow category.** Don't call yourself a "marketing agency" or a "business coach." Narrow the category until you're one of very few in it:
- "Business coach" → "schedule growth coach for PT clinics"
- "Sales consultant" → "founder-replacement sales consultant"
- "Marketing agency" → "patient acquisition agency for dental groups"
- "Copywriter" → "email revenue partner for Shopify brands"
- "Financial planner" → "first-decade financial planner for physicians"

### The external versions

**The 10-second version (introductions, bios, the first line of every call):**
> I help `{Buyer}` `{Outcome}` without `{Dread}`.

**The 30-second version (networking, podcast intros, "so what do you do?"):**
> I work with `{Buyer}`. Usually they come to me right after `{Trigger}`. The thing I hear most is `{Pain Phrase}`. We use a method called the `{Mechanism}` to help them `{Outcome}` in about `{Timeframe}`, without `{Dread}`. `{Proof Point}`

**The "only" statement (the one that ends comparisons):**
> We're the only `[narrow category]` that `[the specific thing your Mechanism does that others skip]`.

Examples:
- "We're the only clinic coach who starts with the patients you already have, not with getting more."
- "We're the only sales consultant whose job is to make sure you're never on a sales call again."
- "We're the only dental ads agency that's paid on booked patients, not leads."

Operator note: the "only" statement has to be true. If someone else does the same thing, narrow it further until it's true.

## 1.3 Ideal Client Clarity

The best way to raise your close rate isn't better copy. It's talking to better-fit people. Everything in this subsection is about knowing exactly who those people are, and who they aren't.

### 1.3.1 The Ideal Client Profile: Eight Filters

A buyer is ideal when they pass all eight. Most of your best clients ever would have passed all eight.

1. **They have the problem right now.** Not "would benefit from." Has it, knows it, can describe it. Signal: they use some version of `{Pain Phrase}` unprompted.
2. **They had a trigger in the last 90 days.** Something happened. Signal: you can answer "why now?" for them.
3. **They can afford it without strain.** The outcome is worth at least 10× your Build price to them in year one. If your Build is $9,000, they should see at least $90,000 of value. Signal: their revenue, income or budget is at least 15× your Build price.
4. **They have the authority.** They can sign, or the person who can sign is on the call. Signal: they say "I" when talking about the decision, not "we'll need to see."
5. **They've tried something already.** People who've tried `{Old Way}` and failed understand the problem. People who've tried nothing are still hoping it goes away. Signal: they can name what they tried.
6. **They can give the time.** About two hours a week for 90 days. Signal: they don't flinch at the number.
7. **They're coachable.** They want a method, not a validator. Signal: they ask "how" questions, not just "how much."
8. **You can actually deliver the result for them.** Their situation fits your mechanism. Signal: the Diagnostic produces three or more clear fixes.

### 1.3.2 The Qualification Scorecard

Use this after every discovery conversation and before every Diagnostic. Score each filter 0, 1 or 2.

| Filter | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Has the problem now | Vague or theoretical | Admits it when asked | Describes it unprompted |
| Recent trigger | None | Over 90 days ago | Within 90 days |
| Can afford it | Under 10× revenue to price | 10× to 15× | Over 15× |
| Authority | Needs others, won't include them | Needs others, will include them | Decides alone |
| Tried something | Nothing | Tried, unclear result | Tried, clearly failed |
| Time available | Under 1 hour a week | 1 to 2 hours | 2 or more hours |
| Coachable | Defensive | Neutral | Curious, asks "how" |
| Fit with mechanism | Poor | Partial | Clear |

**Scoring:**
- **13 to 16:** Ideal. Offer the Diagnostic on the call.
- **9 to 12:** Good. Offer the Diagnostic, but expect a longer decision.
- **5 to 8:** Not yet. Offer the lead magnet or Self-Install Kit. Put them in the email sequence.
- **0 to 4:** Not a fit. Refer them out (1.3.5).

### 1.3.3 The Trigger Library

Twenty-four triggers that start buying cycles in service businesses. Find the five that apply to your `{Buyer}` and build your outreach, content and ads around them.

**Loss triggers (strongest)**
1. Lost their biggest client or account
2. A key employee quit or gave notice
3. A bad month: revenue dropped 20% or more
4. A competitor opened nearby or undercut them
5. A partnership or referral source dried up
6. Got a bad review, audit or complaint that stung

**Growth triggers**
7. Just opened a new location, office or product line
8. Just hired, or is about to hire, their first or second employee in a role
9. Hit a revenue milestone and the old way stopped working
10. Raised money or took on debt and now has a number to hit
11. Got a big contract that stretches their capacity
12. Doubled the team in 12 months and things are breaking

**Money triggers**
13. A vendor raised prices (ads, rent, software, insurance)
14. Tax bill or year-end numbers came in worse than expected
15. Margins dropped and they can't tell why
16. Ad costs or customer acquisition costs jumped

**Deadline triggers**
17. Start of a new quarter or fiscal year
18. A planning meeting, board meeting or investor update is coming
19. A lease renewal, contract renewal or license renewal is coming
20. A seasonal peak is 60 to 90 days away

**Personal and comparison triggers**
21. A peer or competitor visibly pulled ahead
22. A health scare, burnout or family event forced a rethink
23. They just took a vacation and the business fell apart without them
24. They turned an age or hit a business anniversary that made them take stock

### 1.3.4 Harvesting Their Language

Your `{Pain Phrase}` and every headline in Section 2 should come from words real buyers use. Here's where to find them, and how to collect them on calls.

**Where to look:**
- Your last 10 sales call recordings or notes. The first five minutes of each one, especially.
- 1-star, 2-star and 3-star reviews of your competitors. 1-star tells you the pain. 3-star tells you what's still missing after they bought.
- Reddit, Facebook groups and forums where your buyer complains. Search "[industry] + frustrated," "[industry] + anyone else," "[industry] + is it just me."
- Questions in your inbox and DMs, word for word.
- Your own Diagnostic intake forms (once you're running them).

**Five questions that pull out language on calls:**
1. "Walk me through what made you book this call this week, instead of last month."
2. "If you had to explain the problem to your spouse at dinner, what would you say?"
3. "What have you already tried? What happened?"
4. "If we fixed this, what's the first thing that would be different on a Monday morning?"
5. "What would make this a waste of money for you?"

**The capture rule:** write their answers down word for word in a running document called the Language Bank. Don't clean up the grammar. The rougher it is, the better it converts.

### 1.3.5 Who Signs vs. Who Feels It

In many businesses, the person who feels the pain isn't the person who pays. When those are different people, you need two messages.

- **The person who feels it** wants the pain to stop. Speak to their day: the hours, the frustration, the 9pm emails.
- **The person who signs** wants the numbers to work and the risk to be low. Speak to their math: the cost of the problem, the return, the guarantee.

Example (Dental Ads Agency): the office manager feels the empty schedule. The owner dentist signs. The office manager gets the Diagnostic invitation framed as "this will make your phones ring." The owner gets the Findings Report framed as "this is $14,000 a month you're paying for and not getting."

Operator note: when the signer isn't on the call, use the Champion Email in Section 9 (Part Two) to arm the person who is.

### 1.3.6 The Anti-Avatar

These are the people who will cost you more than they pay you. Spotting them early is worth more than any closing technique.

**1. The Bargain Hunter.**
Red flag: the first question is about price, and the second is about discounts.
What it costs you: they'll question every invoice and leave the moment someone cheaper shows up.

**2. The Rescuer Seeker.**
Red flag: the business is weeks away from closing and they need a miracle.
What it costs you: you're being hired to fix a cash crisis, which your offer doesn't solve. They'll blame you for not saving them.

**3. The Researcher.**
Red flag: has talked to six providers, wants a detailed proposal from each, and "just wants to understand the options."
What it costs you: hours of free consulting that become someone else's plan.

**4. The Delegator Who Won't Delegate.**
Red flag: wants it "done for them" but insists on approving every comma.
What it costs you: your done-for-you hours double and your timeline slips.

**5. The Validation Seeker.**
Red flag: has already decided what to do and wants you to agree.
What it costs you: they'll ignore the Blueprint and blame the result on you.

**6. The Absent Owner.**
Red flag: can't find 2 hours a week, misses the Diagnostic once already.
What it costs you: the guarantee gets triggered by your client's no-shows, and you work 60 free days.

**7. The Wrong Stage.**
Red flag: revenue or volume is far below the threshold where your mechanism works.
What it costs you: you'll do good work that can't produce a good result.

**8. The Serial Program Buyer.**
Red flag: has bought five coaching programs in two years and "didn't really get into" any of them.
What it costs you: low completion, low results, no testimonial.

**9. The Committee.**
Red flag: four people need to approve, none of them are on the call, and nobody owns the decision.
What it costs you: a 90-day sales cycle for a 90-day engagement.

**10. The Hostile Ex-Client (of someone else).**
Red flag: spends 20 minutes on how terrible their last provider was.
What it costs you: you're the next story they tell.

**The decline script (kind, clear, and it protects your reputation):**

> "I appreciate you walking me through all of that. Based on what you've shared, I don't think we're the right fit right now, and I'd rather tell you that today than take your money and have it not work. Here's what I'd suggest instead: `[specific alternative: a lower-cost resource, a different provider type, or a thing to fix first]`. If `[the condition that would make them a fit]` changes, I'd genuinely like to hear from you."

### 1.4.4 How the Offers Connect (the rules)

1. **Every step credits toward the next.** The Self-Install Kit credits toward the Diagnostic. The Diagnostic credits toward the Build. Credits have a deadline (30 days and 14 days), which creates real, honest urgency.
2. **Every step ends by naming the next.** The Kit ends with a Diagnostic invitation. The Diagnostic ends with the Build proposal. The Build ends with the Partner proposal.
3. **Never present more than one next step at a time.** A confused buyer buys nothing.
4. **Never ask for the next step before the current one has delivered a win.** The Partner conversation happens after the Day-90 numbers are on the screen, not before.
5. **The ladder isn't a funnel to push people through. It's a set of doors.** Some buyers will walk straight into Build + Run from a referral. Let them.

## 1.5 Pricing Psychology and Anchoring

Price is a story. The same number feels cheap or expensive depending on what the buyer was looking at right before they saw it. These are the principles, with the exact words to use.

### 1.5.1 Anchor to the cost of the problem, out loud

Before you ever say your price, say what the problem costs. In dollars. Using their numbers.

**The cost-of-problem script (calls and Diagnostic reviews):**

> "Let's put a number on this before we talk about fixing it. You told me `[their input 1]` and `[their input 2]`. That means this is costing you roughly `{Cost of Problem}`. Over the next 12 months, if nothing changes, that's `[12 × Cost of Problem]`. Does that number feel right to you, or is it higher?"

Then stop talking. Let them sit with it. If they say "higher," write their number down and use it from now on.

**The worksheet (put this in your Diagnostic Findings Report):**

| Line | Their number |
|---|---|
| A. The thing that's leaking (empty slots, unbooked leads, stalled deals, unworked list) | |
| B. The value of each unit (average visit, average patient value, average deal, revenue per subscriber) | |
| C. Monthly cost: A × B | |
| D. Annual cost: C × 12 | |
| E. Three-year cost: D × 3 | |
| F. The investment to fix it | `{Core Price}` |
| G. Months to pay it back: F ÷ C | |

When G is under three months, the price objection usually disappears on its own.

### 1.5.2 Present the highest tier first

Show Build + Run first, then Build, then Blueprint. The first number they see becomes the reference point. After seeing 2.2× `{Core Price}`, the Build price feels like the sensible middle, which it is.

**The script:**

> "There are three ways to do this. The most complete is Build + Run, where we build it, install it and run it for you for six months. That's `[price]`. Most clients choose Build: same build and install, with us beside you for the full 90 days, then you run it. That's `{Core Price}`. And if your team's strong and you mainly need the plan, there's Blueprint at `[price]`. Based on what you've told me, I'd recommend Build. Here's why..."

Always recommend one. Buyers want to be told which one you'd pick for them.

### 1.5.3 Discount the term, never the price

If you discount the price, you've told the buyer the original price was made up. Instead:
- **Pay in full** is the best deal (the three-payment plan costs 8% more).
- **Founding Client pricing** is a real, disclosed reason, with a cap (first five clients) and an exchange (detailed feedback and results write-up).
- **Credits** reward speed (Diagnostic credited within 14 days).
- **Add, don't subtract.** If someone needs a nudge, add a bonus (an extra emergency call, a team seat), never cut the price.

### 1.5.4 The one-third rule

If nobody pushes back on your price, it's too low. A healthy offer gets a price objection from roughly a third of qualified prospects. Track it (Section 7).

**The raise protocol:**
- After every five Build sales, raise `{Core Price}` by 10% to 15%.
- Keep raising until your Diagnostic-to-Build close rate drops below 35%, or until more than half of qualified prospects push back on price.
- When either happens, hold the price for ten more sales, sharpen the proof and the outcome, then test again.

### 1.5.5 Use round numbers for premium, specific numbers for low-ticket

- Diagnostic, Build, Partner: round numbers. $950, $9,000, $2,000. Round numbers read as confident and professional.
- Self-Install Kit and order bumps: specific numbers. $297, $197. Specific numbers read as a calculated deal, which fits a low-ticket product.
- Never use $9,997 for a high-ticket service. It reads as an infomarketer and costs you trust with exactly the buyer you want.

### 1.5.6 Show the price as a fraction of the problem

> "The Build is `{Core Price}`. That's less than `[X weeks]` of what this problem is costing you right now."

Calculate X weeks: `{Core Price}` ÷ (`{Cost of Problem}` ÷ 4.3). For the Clinic Coach: $7,500 ÷ ($18,000 ÷ 4.3) = 1.8 weeks. "Less than two weeks of what this is costing you." That's the sentence that closes.

### 1.5.7 Name the alternative's price

Your real competitor is usually the buyer's alternative, not another provider. Price it out loud.

> "The other way to solve this is to hire `[the role]`. Fully loaded, that's about `[salary × 1.3]` a year, plus three to six months before they're productive, plus the risk they leave. The Build is `{Core Price}`, it's done in 90 days, and the system stays when people leave."

Reference costs to adapt:
- Hiring a marketing manager: $75,000 to $95,000 salary, about $100,000 to $125,000 fully loaded.
- Hiring a sales rep: $60,000 base plus commission, about $90,000 to $130,000 fully loaded.
- Hiring a full-time CMO or VP Sales: $200,000 to $300,000 fully loaded.
- Doing it yourself: your hourly value × the hours. A founder worth $300 an hour spending 10 hours a week for 6 months is $78,000 of their time.

### 1.5.8 Charge for the Diagnostic, always

A free strategy session is worth exactly what it costs. A paid Diagnostic:
- Pre-qualifies (anyone who pays $950 to look at a problem is serious about solving it).
- Raises show rates (paid sessions show up at 90%+; free calls often show at 50% to 70%).
- Changes the frame. You're a professional doing paid work, not a salesperson auditioning.

**The script when someone asks "can we just do a free call first?":**

### 1.5.9 Present the price once, clearly, then stop

Say the number. Don't apologize, don't justify, don't fill the silence. The first person to speak after a price is presented usually gives something away. Let it be them.

**Wrong:** "So it's $9,000, which I know sounds like a lot, but honestly when you think about it..."
**Right:** "The Build is $9,000, or three payments of $3,240." *(Stop.)*

### 1.5.10 Make the payment plan a little more expensive

# Section 4: Upsell, Downsell and Order Bump Architecture

A single offer leaves money on the table in two directions. Buyers who want more have nowhere to go. Buyers who want less walk away with nothing. The stack in this section fixes both, so every buyer finds the right next step, at the right moment, without feeling sold to.

---

## 4.0 The Stack at a Glance

Here's every offer in the system, when it appears and what triggers it.

| Moment | Offer | Default price | Type | Trigger |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales page / email, said "not now" | Self-Install Kit | $297 | Downsell | Visited booking page, didn't book |
| Diagnostic checkout | Team Debrief Add-On | 30% of `{Diagnostic Price}` | Order bump | Checkbox at checkout |
| Right after Diagnostic payment | Implementation Sprint | 100% of `{Diagnostic Price}` | One-time upsell | Thank-you page |
| Diagnostic review | Blueprint / Build / Build + Run | See 1.1.5 | Core sale | End of Diagnostic session |
| Said no to Build and Blueprint | Session Pack | 20% of `{Core Price}` | Downsell | End of Diagnostic review or follow-up |
| Day 45 to 60 of the Build | Build + Run upgrade | Difference, credited | Mid-engagement upsell | Quick-Win and Phase 2 going well |
| Any time in the Build | Premium add-ons | See 4.5 | Add-on | A specific need comes up |
| Day 75 to 90 of the Build | `{Brand}` Partner | `{Partner Price}` | Continuity | Day-90 Results Review |
| Said no to Partner | Quarterly Tune-Up | 1× `{Partner Price}` per quarter | Downsell | Day-90 review or 30 days after |

**The principle under all of it:** every upsell is the next logical step toward the same outcome. Every downsell is less scope, never the same scope for less money. If you ever find yourself offering the same thing for a lower price, you've told the buyer your prices were made up.

## 4.1 The Order Bump: Team Debrief Add-On

### What it is

A 45-minute session, held within seven days of the Diagnostic, where `{Founder}` walks the buyer's team (or partner, or key staff) through the findings: what's leaking, why it matters and what the plan is. Recorded, with a one-page summary for anyone who couldn't attend.

### Why this bump, specifically

- **It's useful for the buyer.** Owners almost always struggle to explain the findings to their team with the same clarity they heard them. This solves that.
- **It raises your Build conversion.** A team that's heard the diagnosis from you is a team that wants it fixed. The owner stops being the only person pushing.
- **It kills an objection early.** "My team won't buy into this" is one of the top reasons a Diagnostic doesn't convert. The bump handles it before it comes up.
- **It's cheap to deliver.** 45 minutes plus 15 minutes of summary, using the prep work you've already done.

### Pricing

30% of `{Diagnostic Price}`, rounded to a specific number ending in 7. On a $950 Diagnostic, that's $297. On $2,500, it's $747. On $600, it's $197.

The single most useful number for running this business: **total revenue from a cohort of Diagnostics ÷ number of Diagnostics.** In the example above, that's $138,232 ÷ 20 = $6,912 per Diagnostic.

If each Diagnostic is worth $6,912 over its lifetime, spending $500 in ads or outreach to book one is an easy decision. Most service businesses never know this number, so they never feel confident spending to grow.

## 4.8 The Rules That Keep the Stack Clean

1. **One offer at a time.** Never present two upsells in the same moment. A buyer choosing between three options and an add-on buys nothing.
2. **Win first, then offer.** No upsell before the current offer has produced a visible result. The Sprint follows payment because the Diagnostic itself is about to deliver. Partner follows Day 90 because the number is on the screen.
3. **Every upsell moves toward the same outcome.** If an add-on isn't about the result they bought, it's a distraction. Save it.
4. **Credits have deadlines.** Credits create honest urgency. Without a deadline, they create indefinite delay.
5. **Downsells cut scope, never price.** Same scope for less money teaches every future buyer to negotiate.
6. **Every "no" gets one alternative, then a graceful exit.** Offer one downsell. If they say no again, thank them and stop. The report's theirs, the relationship stays good, and they'll come back when they're ready.
7. **Write it down.** Every bump, upsell, upgrade and downsell is listed in your client agreement template with its price and terms, so there's never a surprise invoice and never a "you didn't tell me."
8. **Track every step separately.** Bump take rate, Sprint take rate, Diagnostic-to-Build rate, tier mix, Partner continuation rate, average Partner length. Section 7 gives you the tracker.

# Section 5: Competitive Blindspot and Positioning Assets

Your buyer is comparing you to something. Usually not who you think. Most of the time it's three options at once: another provider, hiring someone, and doing it themselves. This section shows you how to find what each of those options is missing, and how to say it so the buyer sees the gap without you ever having to knock anyone.

---

## 5.0 The Rule Before Everything Else

**Compare categories, never companies.** You never say "XYZ Agency is bad." You say "most agencies report clicks instead of patients." The buyer fills in the name. You look like the professional in the room, and you never get dragged into an argument you can't win.

Two more rules:

1. **Every claim you make about the alternatives must be true for most of them.** If a buyer can name three competitors who already do the thing you say "most" don't, you've lost credibility you won't get back.
2. **Every gap you point out must be one you actually fill.** Pointing out a weakness you share is the fastest way to lose a sale.

---

## 5.1 The Blindspot Audit

A blindspot is something your competitors don't do, don't say, or do badly, that your buyer cares about. You find them on purpose, once a quarter, in about three hours.

### Step 1: Pick your five comparisons

Don't just study your direct competitors. Study the full set of things your buyer considers.

1. **Direct competitor A:** the one your buyers mention most.
2. **Direct competitor B:** the biggest or best-known name in your space.
3. **Direct competitor C:** the cheapest credible option.
4. **The hire:** the employee or in-house role your buyer could bring on instead (marketing manager, sales rep, operations lead, bookkeeper).
5. **Do-it-yourself:** the course, software, template or free advice your buyer could use alone.

Operator note: the fastest way to find out who you're really up against is to ask. Add this question to your Diagnostic intake: "Who else did you consider, or what else did you try, before booking this?" After ten answers, you'll know your real competition better than any research could tell you.

### Step 2: Fill in the Competitor Teardown Sheet

One sheet per comparison. Use only public information: their website, pricing pages, content, reviews, job postings and anything your own clients tell you about their experience. Don't pose as a buyer to get information. It's dishonest, and if it comes out, it's the story people tell about you.

**The Competitor Teardown Sheet (12 fields):**

| Field | What to capture |
|---|---|
| 1. Who they say they serve | Their exact words. Broad or narrow? |
| 2. Their main promise | The headline result. Is it measurable? |
| 3. Their mechanism | Do they name a method? Explain why it works? |
| 4. What they actually deliver | Activity (calls, posts, reports) or outcome (a number moved)? |
| 5. Pricing | Shown or hidden? Structure? Approximate range? |
| 6. Contract terms | Length, minimums, cancellation |
| 7. Guarantee | None, vague, or specific? |
| 8. First step | Free call, form, paid entry, course? |
| 9. Proof | Named results with numbers, vague testimonials, logos, nothing? |
| 10. What 1-star to 3-star reviews complain about | Exact phrases |
| 11. What 5-star reviews praise | Exact phrases |
| 12. The gap | The one thing buyers want that this option doesn't give them |

**Where to find reviews for service businesses:** Google Business Profile, Clutch, G2 (for agencies and consultants who serve software companies), Trustpilot, Yelp, Facebook page reviews, Reddit threads that mention them by name, and the "recommendations" section of LinkedIn profiles.

### Step 3: Check for the twelve common blindspots

Across coaching, consulting, agency, freelance and local professional services, the same twelve gaps show up over and over. For each one: how to spot it, and how to turn it into an advantage.

**Blindspot 1: They sell activity, not outcomes.**
- How to spot it: their deliverables are verbs and counts ("4 calls a month," "12 posts," "weekly reports") instead of results ("cost per patient under $250").
- How to use it: lead with the number you move. "We don't sell calls. We sell `{Outcome}`."

**Blindspot 2: They serve everyone.**
- How to spot it: their homepage says "businesses," "brands," "entrepreneurs" or lists eight industries.
- How to use it: say who you serve in the first five words of everything. "Built for `{Buyer}`. Not for everyone."

**Blindspot 3: No named method.**
- How to spot it: they describe what they do with generic words ("strategy," "best practices," "proven process") and never explain why it works.
- How to use it: name and explain your `{Mechanism}` in two sentences, everywhere.

**Blindspot 4: Hidden pricing.**
- How to spot it: "contact us for pricing," "custom quotes," no numbers anywhere.
- How to use it: publish your Diagnostic price and your Build range. Buyers trust the provider who goes first on price. "Here's exactly what it costs. No sales call needed to find out."

**Blindspot 5: No guarantee, or a vague one.**
- How to spot it: nothing about what happens if it doesn't work, or "satisfaction guaranteed."
- How to use it: lead with your two specific guarantees. "If you do your part and we don't hit the target, we keep going for free."

**Blindspot 6: Free calls as the first step.**
- How to spot it: every button says "Book a free strategy call."
- How to use it: frame the paid Diagnostic as the more serious option. "Free calls are sales calls. The Diagnostic is real work, with a written report you keep."

**Blindspot 7: They stop at advice.**
- How to spot it: deliverables are plans, recommendations, audits, strategy sessions. Nothing gets built.
- How to use it: "Most consultants hand you a plan. We build it with you."

**Blindspot 8: They don't install or train.**
- How to spot it: no mention of team training, procedures or handoff.
- How to use it: "When we finish, your team runs it. Recorded training, written procedures, and a system that stays when people leave."

**Blindspot 9: Long contracts.**
- How to spot it: 6 or 12-month minimums, especially common with agencies.
- How to use it: "A fixed 90-day engagement with a finish line. No 12-month lock-in."

**Blindspot 10: They report vanity numbers.**
- How to spot it: case studies and reports focus on impressions, clicks, followers, open rates, "engagement."
- How to use it: "We report one kind of number: the kind that shows up in your bank account."

**Blindspot 11: Slow and hard to reach.**
- How to spot it: reviews that mention "hard to get hold of," "slow to respond," "we never heard from them."
- How to use it: make your Direct Access promise specific. "Any business day, answers within one business day."

**Blindspot 12: The founder sells, juniors deliver.**
- How to spot it: reviews saying "great at first, then we got passed off," team pages full of junior titles, job postings for "account coordinators."
- How to use it (only if true): "You work with `{Founder}`. Not a junior account manager."

### Step 4: Score and choose

For each blindspot, score how often it shows up across your five comparisons (0 to 5) and how much your buyer cares about it (1 to 3, based on your Language Bank and reviews). Multiply.

The top three scores are your positioning. Build your headlines, your "only" statement (Part One, 1.2) and your ad angles (Section 9) around them.

**Example (Dental Ads Agency):**

| Blindspot | Frequency (0 to 5) | Buyer cares (1 to 3) | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Report vanity numbers | 5 | 3 | 15 |
| Long contracts | 4 | 3 | 12 |
| Founder sells, juniors deliver | 4 | 2 | 8 |
| Hidden pricing | 5 | 1 | 5 |

Top three: vanity reporting, long contracts, juniors delivering. So the positioning becomes: "Paid on booked patients, not clicks. 60 days, no 12-month contract. And you work with the person who built the system."

## 5.2 Messaging Angles That Take Market Share

Twelve angles. Each is a way of framing your offer against the alternatives. Use the top three from your Blindspot Audit as your main angles, and keep the rest for ads, content and specific objections.

For each angle: when to use it, the headline, the supporting line and the proof you need for it to land.

### Angle 1: The Specialist

- **When:** competitors serve broad markets (Blindspot 2).
- **Headline:** "Built for `{Buyer}`. Only `{Buyer}`."
- **Supporting line:** "When you only work with one kind of business, you've already seen your problem a hundred times. You don't pay for us to learn your industry."
- **Proof needed:** the number of clients you've served in this niche, or the depth of your experience in it.

### Angle 2: The Outcome

- **When:** competitors sell activity (Blindspot 1).
- **Headline:** "We don't sell hours. We sell `{Outcome}`."
- **Supporting line:** "Every engagement starts with a baseline number and a target number. If we don't move it, we keep going."
- **Proof needed:** at least one before-and-after number.

### Angle 3: The Transparency

- **When:** competitors hide pricing (Blindspot 4).
- **Headline:** "Here's exactly what it costs."
- **Supporting line:** "The Diagnostic is `{Diagnostic Price}`. Most clients invest between `[Blueprint price]` and `[Build + Run price]`. You won't need a sales call to find that out."
- **Proof needed:** your prices, published.

### Angle 4: The Risk Reversal

- **When:** competitors offer no guarantee (Blindspot 5).
- **Headline:** "If it doesn't work, we keep working. For free."
- **Supporting line:** "We set the target together, on your numbers. If you do your part and we miss it, the next 60 days are on us."
- **Proof needed:** the written guarantee in your agreement.

### Angle 5: The Speed

- **When:** competitors take months to show anything, or buyers have been burned by slow results.
- **Headline:** "Your first result in 14 days."

### Building your category of one

## 6.2 Content Pillars

Five pillars, each tied directly to the offer. Every piece of content you publish should sit in one of these, and every pillar points toward the Leak Check or the Diagnostic.

### Pillar 1: The Leak (about 30% of content)

- **What it covers:** the hidden problems behind the visible symptoms. Where results leak in a `{Buyer}` business, and how to spot it.
- **Why it sells:** it's the core idea behind your `{Mechanism}`. Every leak you describe is a small Diagnostic, and it makes the reader wonder what you'd find in theirs.
- **Formats that work:** "the real reason ___," short case breakdowns, checklists, "signs you have this leak."
- **Call to action:** take the Leak Check.

### Pillar 2: The Math (about 20%)

- **What it covers:** the numbers. What problems cost, how to calculate it, what good looks like, which numbers to track.
- **Why it sells:** a problem with a price on it gets solved. A problem without one gets put off. This pillar puts prices on problems.
- **Formats that work:** worked examples, calculators, "the 3 numbers every `[buyer type]` should know," benchmark posts.
- **Call to action:** do the 3-Number Check (Leak Check, page 2).

### Pillar 3: The Method (about 20%)

- **What it covers:** how you fix things. The order, the principles, pieces of the `{Mechanism}`, how a Quick-Win Fix works.
- **Why it sells:** people believe what they understand. Teaching the method raises perceived likelihood of success (Part One, 1.1.2) more than any testimonial.
- **Formats that work:** step-by-step posts, "how we'd fix ___," frameworks, before/after process breakdowns.
- **Call to action:** book a Diagnostic to have it applied to your numbers.

### Pillar 4: The Proof (about 15%)

- **What it covers:** results, behind the scenes, client stories, what a Diagnostic actually looks like.
- **Why it sells:** it shows the method working for someone like them.
- **Formats that work:** case studies, anonymized Findings Report walkthroughs, "week 3 of a Build," client quotes with numbers.
- **Call to action:** book a Diagnostic.

### Pillar 5: The Point of View (about 15%)

- **What it covers:** what you believe about your industry that others don't. Your disagreements. What you'd change.
- **Why it sells:** it's what makes people follow you instead of just reading you. It also attracts the buyers who agree and repels the ones who'd be a bad fit.
- **Formats that work:** contrarian takes, "what I'd tell a new `[buyer type]`," industry predictions, "unpopular opinion."
- **Call to action:** reply with your view, or take the Leak Check.

# Section 7: Revenue Leakage Closure Elements

Most service businesses don't have a lead problem. They have a leak problem. Money comes in the top and drains out of a dozen small holes: slow replies, no-shows, proposals nobody follows up, clients who leave because nobody offered them a next step. Closing these is usually the fastest revenue available, because you've already paid to get the people this far.

This section lists the leaks, gives you the rewritten assets that close them, and gives you the tracker that proves it worked.

---

## 7.1 The Eighteen Leaks

For each leak: the symptom, how to check for it in under ten minutes, a quick way to estimate what it costs, and the fix.

### Conversion leaks (fix these first)

**Leak 1: Slow speed to lead.**
- **Symptom:** inquiries sit for hours or days before anyone replies.
- **Check:** look at your last 20 inquiries. How long until the first reply? Average it.
- **Cost estimate:** inquiries that wait more than a day convert far less often than ones answered within the hour. If you get 20 inquiries a month and slow replies cost you even 2 clients, multiply 2 by your average client value.
- **Fix:** an instant automated reply (7.2.7), plus a personal reply within four business hours. Put inquiry alerts on your phone.

**Leak 2: No-shows.**
- **Symptom:** booked calls where nobody turns up.
- **Check:** booked calls in the last 90 days vs. calls that happened.
- **Cost estimate:** no-shows × your close rate × average client value.
- **Fix:** the pre-session reminder sequence (Part One, 2.6.4), a paid Diagnostic (paid sessions show up far more reliably) and the no-show rescue sequence (7.2.8).

**Leak 3: Proposals without follow-up.**
- **Symptom:** you send a proposal, hear nothing, and move on.
- **Check:** proposals sent in the last six months. How many got three or more follow-ups?
- **Cost estimate:** open proposals × a conservative recovery rate (10% to 20%) × proposal value.
- **Fix:** the proposal follow-up sequence (7.2.6). Never send a proposal without a booked time to review it together.

**Leak 4: Free discovery calls.**
- **Symptom:** you spend 5 to 15 hours a month on free calls with people who never buy.
- **Check:** free calls last month vs. clients closed from them.
- **Cost estimate:** hours spent on calls that didn't close × your hourly value.
- **Fix:** replace the free strategy call with a 20-minute fit call (Section 9) and a paid Diagnostic (Part One).

**Leak 5: "Let me think about it."**
- **Symptom:** prospects end calls undecided and drift away.
- **Check:** in your last 10 sales conversations, how many ended without a yes, a no or a booked next step?
- **Cost estimate:** stalled prospects × your normal close rate × client value.
- **Fix:** always end with a decision or a booked next step. Use the "think about it" script (7.2.10) and the objection library (Section 9).

**Leak 6: Vague offer.**
- **Symptom:** buyers ask "so what exactly do I get?" or compare you to cheaper options that aren't the same thing.
- **Check:** can you state your offer in the one-sentence test (Part One, 1.0)? Can a past client repeat it back?
- **Fix:** the full offer architecture in Part One, Section 1.

### Page and message leaks

**Leak 7: The homepage talks about you.**
- **Symptom:** your headline is your company name, a slogan or "Welcome."
- **Check:** read your homepage's first line. Does it name the buyer and the outcome?
- **Fix:** the rewritten homepage hero (7.2.1).

**Leak 8: Too many calls to action.**
- **Symptom:** your homepage has five buttons: "Learn more," "Our services," "Contact us," "Read our blog," "Book a call."
- **Check:** count the different actions on your homepage.
- **Fix:** one primary action (book the Diagnostic) and one secondary (take the Leak Check). Remove the rest or move them into the menu.

**Leak 9: Weak button text.**
- **Symptom:** buttons that say "Submit," "Learn more," "Contact."
- **Fix:** the button rewrites (7.2.4).

**Leak 10: Hidden prices.**
- **Symptom:** "contact us for pricing" everywhere.
- **Fix:** publish the Diagnostic price and the Build range (Part One, 1.5.12).

**Leak 11: No proof near the decision.**
- **Symptom:** testimonials sit on a separate page nobody visits.
- **Check:** is there a result with a number within one scroll of every button?
- **Fix:** put one specific result directly above or below every call to action.

### Delivery and retention leaks

**Leak 12: A silent first 48 hours.**
- **Symptom:** a client pays and hears nothing personal for days.
- **Fix:** the first-48-hours protocol (Part One, 1.4.3).

**Leak 13: Scope creep.**
- **Symptom:** you regularly do work you didn't price.
- **Check:** for your last three projects, compare hours estimated to hours worked.
- **Cost estimate:** extra hours × your hourly rate.
- **Fix:** the scope-creep script and Done-For-You Hour Blocks (Part One, 4.5).

**Leak 14: No offer when the project ends.**
- **Symptom:** clients finish, say thanks, and leave.
- **Check:** what percentage of clients bought anything after their first engagement?
- **Cost estimate:** clients finishing per year × a conservative continuation rate (30%) × 8 months × your monthly continuity price.
- **Fix:** the Partner offer and Day-90 conversation (Part One, 4.4).

**Leak 15: Discounting.**
- **Symptom:** you regularly knock 10% to 20% off to close.
- **Check:** average price paid vs. list price over your last 10 clients.
- **Cost estimate:** the difference, added up.
- **Fix:** discount term, never price (Part One, 1.5.3). Use the price-objection scripts in Section 9.

**Leak 16: Prices haven't moved in over a year.**
- **Symptom:** you're charging the same as 12 months ago while costs, skill and results have gone up.
- **Fix:** the price raise protocol (Part One, 1.5.4) and the price increase letter (7.2.11).

### Expansion leaks

**Leak 17: No referral ask.**
- **Symptom:** referrals happen by accident, if at all.
- **Fix:** the referral request (Section 6.5.10) sent at a set point after every good result.

**Leak 18: A dormant past-client and lead list.**
- **Symptom:** hundreds of past clients, old leads and contacts who haven't heard from you in months.
- **Check:** how many people have you talked to about working together in the last three years who aren't clients now?
- **Fix:** the reactivation emails (Section 6.5.6 and 7.2.9).

### 7.2.8 The no-show rescue sequence

**Next business day (email):**

> **Subject:** one last try

> `{Founder}`

> **Subject:** `[their problem, in their words]`
>
> Hi `[First name]`, are you still dealing with `[their problem]`?
>
> `{Founder}`

**Option B (the update email):**
> **Subject:** something changed on my side
>
> Hi `[First name]`,
>

>
> Is `[their problem]` still something you'd like to fix?
>
> `{Founder}`

Operator note: Option A looks almost too simple. Send it anyway. Short, personal questions get replies, and every reply is a sales conversation you didn't have to start from zero.

### 7.2.10 Sales conversation rewrites

**The opening question**

Before: "So, what are you looking for?" *(Puts the buyer on the spot and invites a vague answer.)*

After: "Before we get into anything, what made you book this call this week, instead of last month or next month?" *(Finds the trigger immediately.)*

**Finding the cost**

Before: "That sounds frustrating." *(True, but it moves nothing.)*

After: "Let's put a rough number on it. How many `[units]` do you think you're losing a month to this? And what's each one worth to you?" *(Turns frustration into a figure.)*

**Presenting the price**

Before: "So the investment is $9,000, which I know is a lot, but..." *(Apologizes for the price.)*

After: "The Build is $9,000, or three payments of $3,240. Based on what you've told me, it pays for itself in about `[F ÷ C]` weeks." *(Then stop talking.)*

**The "let me think about it" moment**

Before: "Sure, no problem. Just let me know!" *(The deal usually ends here.)*

After:
> "Of course. It's a real decision and it should get real thought. Can I ask one thing so I can help you think it through? When you say you want to think about it, is it the investment, the timing, or whether this will actually work for you? `[Listen.]` That's helpful. `[Address the specific concern using Section 9.]` Would it help if we booked 15 minutes on `[day]` to go over anything that comes up? That way you're not left hanging, and neither am I."

**Ending every call**

Before: "Great talking to you, I'll follow up!"

After: "So the next step is `[specific step]`, and we've got `[day and time]` booked for it. I'll send `[specific thing]` by `[time]`. Anything I've missed?"

### 7.2.11 The price increase letter

For existing clients on ongoing work, when you raise prices.

> **Subject:** a change to your rate, effective `[date 60 days out]`
>
> Hi `[First name]`,
>
> I wanted to tell you directly and early: starting `[date]`, the rate for `[service]` will go from `[old price]` to `[new price]`.
>
> Here's why. Over the past `[period]`, `[a concrete reason: "the work has expanded to include ___," "our results have improved, with clients averaging ___," "the cost of delivering at the level you expect has risen"]`. The new rate lets me keep doing this properly.
>
> Because you've been with me since `[start date]`, your current rate stays in place until `[date]`, which gives you `[60 or 90]` days to plan for it. And if you'd like to lock in a full year at your current rate, you can do that by paying annually before `[date]`.
>
> If you'd like to talk about it, I'm happy to. Thank you for being a client. I mean that.
>
> `{Founder}`

## 7.3 The Measurement Checklist

You can't fix what you don't measure, and you can't prove a fix worked without a before number. This is the simplest tracking setup that tells you what's working.

### 7.3.1 The baseline rule

**Before you change anything, write down the current number.** Every fix in this system has a baseline. Take it the week before you make the change, and date it. Without a baseline, you'll never know whether the change worked, and you won't have a before-and-after story to tell.

### 7.3.2 The fourteen numbers

**Weekly (track every Monday, 15 minutes):**

1. **New leads:** Leak Check downloads plus direct inquiries.
2. **Speed to lead:** average hours from inquiry to first personal reply. Target: under 4 business hours.
3. **Fit calls booked and held:** and the show rate (held ÷ booked). Target: over 80%.
4. **Diagnostics sold:** count and revenue.
5. **Diagnostic show rate:** target over 90%.
6. **Builds sold:** count, tier and revenue.
7. **Cash collected:** total, this week.

**Monthly (track on the first business day, 30 minutes):**

8. **Lead to Diagnostic rate:** Diagnostics sold ÷ new leads.
9. **Diagnostic to Build rate:** Builds sold ÷ Diagnostics held. Your single most important conversion number.
10. **Order bump and Sprint take rates:** takes ÷ Diagnostics sold.
11. **Price objection rate:** qualified prospects who pushed back on price ÷ qualified prospects who heard it. Healthy: about a third (Part One, 1.5.4).
12. **Average revenue per Diagnostic:** all revenue traced to a month's Diagnostics ÷ number of Diagnostics.
13. **Partner continuation rate:** Build clients who continued ÷ Build clients who finished.
14. **Revenue by source:** which channel (referral, content, outreach, ads, reactivation) produced each sale.

## 8.1 The Priority Order (Fastest Revenue First)

Ranked by how fast each step turns into cash, weighed against the effort it takes. Do them in this order. Don't skip ahead because a later step looks more fun.

**1. Fill in the Token Key and lock the offer.** *(Time: 2 to 3 hours. Revenue impact: makes everything else possible.)*
Use Part One, the Token Key and Section 1. Decide your Diagnostic price, your three Build tiers and your Partner price. Write the one-sentence offer and test it on three past clients.

**2. Set up the Diagnostic checkout.** *(Time: 2 hours.)*
A booking calendar with payment taken at booking (most scheduling tools can do this), the confirmation email, the intake form and the thank-you page (Part One, 2.6). Add the Team Debrief bump if your checkout supports it. If not, leave it for step 9.

**3. Send the warm reactivation email to everyone you know.** *(Time: 3 hours. Revenue impact: often cash within 7 days.)*
Past clients, old leads, people who got proposals, your network. Use Section 6.5.6 and the dead-lead emails in 7.2.9. This is almost always the fastest revenue in the whole system, because these people already trust you.

**4. Follow up every open proposal.** *(Time: 1 to 2 hours.)*
Every proposal sent in the last six months that didn't get a clear no. Use the 1, 2, 3 email (7.2.6). Offer the Diagnostic as a lower-commitment way back in where it fits.

**5. Apply the new pricing to every new conversation from today.** *(Time: none. Revenue impact: immediate on every sale.)*
New prices for new clients starting now. For existing clients, use the price increase letter (7.2.11) with 60 days' notice.

**6. Run your first five Diagnostics and sell from them.** *(Time: about 5 hours per Diagnostic, including prep and report.)*
Use the Diagnostic run-of-show and Build presentation in Section 9. This is where the system starts paying for itself. Your first five will also show you what to fix in the offer.

**7. Publish the sales page.** *(Time: 1 to 2 days.)*
Part One, 2.3. Fill in the tokens, add your proof or the Founding Client block, connect the button to the checkout.

**8. Build the Leak Check and load the 15-email sequence.** *(Time: 1 to 2 days.)*
Section 6.4 and Part One, Section 3. Now every new lead gets nurtured automatically.

**9. Add the order bump and Implementation Sprint upsell.** *(Time: 2 to 3 hours.)*
Part One, 4.1 and 4.2. Small additions that raise revenue on every Diagnostic.

**10. Build the Partner offer and run the Day-90 conversation with current clients.** *(Time: 2 hours to build, then one conversation per client.)*
Part One, 4.4. If you have current clients near the end of an engagement, this step can move above step 7.

**11. Start the content rhythm and outreach.** *(Time: 3 to 5 hours a week, ongoing.)*
Section 6.3, 6.5 and the content calendar in Section 9.

**12. Test paid ads.** *(Time: 3 hours to set up, ongoing.)*
Only after the sales page, Leak Check and sequence are live and you've closed at least three Builds. Section 9 has the ad copy and the test budget.

**Why ads come last:** ads multiply whatever's already there. If your offer, page and follow-up are leaking, ads multiply the leak. Once those are tight, every dollar of ad spend has somewhere good to land.

## 8.6 What Not to Do in the First 30 Days

These are the most common ways people stall out with a system like this.

1. **Don't redesign your website.** Rewrite the words on the pages you have. Design can wait until the offer is proven.
2. **Don't build the Self-Install Kit first.** Low-ticket products are slow to build and slow to pay. Build it in month two or three, if at all.
3. **Don't start ads.** Not until three Builds have closed from the system (8.1, step 12).
4. **Don't change the offer after one "no."** Change it after a pattern: the same objection from three or more qualified buyers.
5. **Don't add bonuses because you're nervous.** If buyers hesitate, sharpen the outcome and proof. Don't pad the stack.
6. **Don't discount to get the first sale.** Use the Founding Client offer if you need early clients. It's a discount with an honest reason and a cap.
7. **Don't wait for everything to be perfect before sending the reactivation emails.** They work with just the Diagnostic checkout live. Send them on Day 7, as planned.
8. **Don't skip the baseline.** Every number you don't record now is a before-and-after story you can't tell later.
9. **Don't try to post on every platform.** Pick the one where your `{Buyer}` actually spends time. Do that one well.
10. **Don't skip the Day-14 and Day-30 reviews.** They're how you find out what to fix, instead of guessing.

## 9.1 Ad and Creative Angles

Operator note: run ads only after the sales page, Leak Check and email sequence are live, and you've closed at least three Builds (Section 8.1, step 12). Ads multiply what's already there.

### 9.1.1 The core ad strategy

For a service offer at this price, the ad's job is almost never to sell the Build. It's to get the right person to take the Leak Check (cold traffic) or book the Diagnostic (warm traffic). The email sequence and the Diagnostic do the selling.

**Two campaigns, that's all:**
1. **Cold:** ads to people who don't know you, offering the free Leak Check. Goal: leads at a sustainable cost.
2. **Warm (retargeting):** ads to people who visited your site, watched your videos or opted in, offering the Diagnostic. Goal: Diagnostics booked.

### 9.1.2 Twelve creative angles

Each angle is a different reason someone stops scrolling. Test three at a time.

1. **The Pain Phrase.** Open with the exact words your buyer says: `{Pain Phrase}`. Works because it feels like you've been listening in.
2. **The Calculator.** "Grab a calculator. This takes four minutes." Show the 3-Number Check. Works because it's useful before it asks for anything.

## 9.3 The Objection and Rebuttal Library

Thirty-two objections, grouped by type, each with a spoken rebuttal. These are written for live conversation. For the written versions, see the sales page FAQ (Part One, 2.3).

### 9.3.1 The four-step method (use it on every objection)

1. **Acknowledge.** Agree with what's true in it. "That's fair." "That makes sense." Never argue with the feeling.
2. **Ask.** Find the real objection behind the stated one. "Can I ask what's behind that?" The first objection is rarely the real one.
3. **Answer.** Answer the real objection, specifically and briefly. One answer, not five.
4. **Advance.** Ask a question that moves things forward. "Does that help?" "So where does that leave you?"

**The rule that matters most:** after you answer, stop talking. Silence after an answer gives the buyer room to decide. Filling it gives them room to doubt.

### 9.3.2 Price objections

**1. "It's too expensive."**
> "That's fair. It's a real investment. Can I ask: too expensive compared to what? `[Listen.]` The way I'd look at it is against the cost of the problem. You told me this is costing about `[monthly cost]` a month. The Build is `{Core Price}`, which is about `[payback]` weeks of that. So the question is really whether you think it'll work. If you're confident it will, it's the cheaper option. If you're not, let's talk about that, because that's the real question."

**2. "I can't afford it right now."**
> "I appreciate you being straight with me. Is it that the money isn't there, or that it's there but it feels like a stretch? `[Listen.]` If it's a stretch, there's a three-payment option, which spreads it over the engagement. If the money genuinely isn't there right now, I'd rather you didn't stretch for this. Blueprint might be a better fit, or taking the plan and running with it. What feels right?"

**3. "Can you do it for less?"**
> "I don't discount the price, because it wouldn't be fair to everyone else who's paid it. What I can do is change the scope. Blueprint is the plan, the Quick-Win Fix and three check-ins, at `[price]`. You and your team do the building. Would that work better?"

## 9.4 The Content Calendar Structure

Content exists to do two jobs: get the right people to the Leak Check, and make the people already on your list more ready to book. This calendar is built around a four-week cycle that lines up with the email offer pushes, so your content and your sales emails are always pulling in the same direction.

### 9.4.1 The weekly rhythm

Three to four pieces a week on your main platform, plus one email to your list.

| Day | Pillar | Format | Call to action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | The Leak (Pillar 1) | Text post or short video | Leak Check |
| Tuesday | The Math (Pillar 2) | Worked example, calculation, carousel | Leak Check |
| Wednesday | The Method or The Proof (Pillars 3 or 4, alternating weeks) | Case breakdown, process post | Diagnostic |
| Thursday | Weekly email to your list | One idea, one call to action | Varies by cycle week |
| Friday (optional) | The Point of View (Pillar 5) | Opinion post | Reply / Leak Check |

### 9.4.2 The four-week cycle

Each month follows the same shape, building toward an offer week.

**Week 1: The Leak.** Content focuses on hidden problems and symptoms vs. causes. Email: a story about a leak you found.
**Week 2: The Math.** Content focuses on costs, numbers and benchmarks. Email: the 3-Number Check, refreshed with a new example.
**Week 3: The Method and Proof.** Content focuses on how you fix things and what happened when you did. Email: a case study or Findings Report walkthrough.
**Week 4: The Offer.** Content keeps teaching, with a clear Diagnostic invitation on two pieces. Email: the three-email offer push (Emails 12, 14 and 15 from Part One, adapted with a fresh early-booking bonus). Run this every four to six weeks, not every month if your list is small.

### 9.4.3 A sample four-week calendar (filled in)

Using the angles from Section 6.3.

| Week | Monday | Tuesday | Wednesday | Thursday (email) | Friday |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Angle 1: "The problem you're trying to fix probably isn't the problem." | Angle 7: "Grab a calculator. This takes four minutes." | Angle 13: "Here's exactly how I'd fix ___ in 90 days." | Story: a leak found in the last place the owner expected (Angle 4) | Angle 25: "Free strategy calls are bad for buyers." |
| 2 | Angle 3: "Five signs you have a leak you can't see." | Angle 8: "`{Cost of Problem}`. That's what this usually costs." | Angle 19: a full case study | The 3-Number Check with a new example | Angle 27: "Stop reporting ___." |
| 3 | Angle 5: "The most expensive step is the one nobody measures." | Angle 9: "The three numbers to check on Monday." | Angle 20: "What a Findings Report actually looks like." | Case study or Findings Report walkthrough | Angle 29: "The fastest-growing ___ all have one thing in common." |
| 4 | Angle 6: "Fix the leak closest to money." | Angle 11: "Why the cheaper option usually costs more." | Angle 14: "The Quick-Win Fix" + Diagnostic invitation | Offer push begins (Email 12) | Angle 30: "Guarantees should be normal. Here's mine." + Diagnostic invitation |

Months two and three: rotate through the remaining angles, then start inventing your own from your Language Bank and recent Diagnostics. After three months, your best-performing posts become your next ad creative (Section 9.1).

### 9.4.4 The repurposing workflow (one long piece, eight short ones)

Each month, create one long piece: a 20 to 30 minute podcast or video, a detailed article or a recorded workshop. Then cut it up:

1. **The full piece** goes on YouTube, your podcast or your blog.
2. **Three short video clips** (30 to 90 seconds each), one per key idea.
3. **Two text posts**, each built from one key point.
4. **One carousel or visual**, built from the main framework or numbers.
5. **One email** to your list, summarizing the main idea with a link.
6. **One quote graphic** with the single best line.

Eight pieces from one recording covers about two weeks of the weekly rhythm.

### 9.4.5 The batching schedule

Content goes faster in batches than one piece at a time.

- **Monthly, 3 hours:** record the long piece and plan the month's calendar.
- **Weekly, 90 minutes (Tuesday, per 8.5):** write and schedule the week's posts and the email.
- **Daily, 15 minutes:** reply to every comment and message. This is where conversations start.

### 9.4.6 The quarterly campaign calendar

Every quarter has one theme tied to your buyer's real calendar. Plan these once a year.

| Quarter | Theme | Why it fits | Offer push timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | "Set the number for the year" | Planning season. Buyers set goals and budgets. | Mid-January and late February |
| Q2 | "Fix the leak before the busy season" | Most service businesses have a summer or mid-year peak. | April and late May |
| Q3 | "Mid-year check: is it working?" | Halfway through the year, buyers compare progress to goals. | July and September |
| Q4 | "Build it before next year" | Buyers want things working by January, and some have budget to use. | October and early November |

Operator note: shift the themes to match your buyer's actual seasons. A tax-focused planner's busy season is not a PT clinic's.

### 9.4.7 What to measure in content

Three numbers, monthly. Ignore the rest.

1. **Leak Check downloads from content** (use a dedicated link per platform).
2. **Diagnostics booked from content** (ask "how did you hear about us?" on the intake).
3. **Replies and direct messages started.** The best leading indicator of a sale.

If a piece gets lots of likes but no downloads or messages, it entertained people without moving them. Make more of the pieces that start conversations.
